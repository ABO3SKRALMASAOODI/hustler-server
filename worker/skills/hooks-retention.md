# hooks-retention — the first two seconds, holding attention, ending on a loop

THE HOOK DECIDES EVERYTHING. Short-form is won or lost before the viewer's thumb moves — roughly 1.5-2.5 seconds. The hook is the single strongest thing the footage contains, moved to position zero:
- FIND it before cutting anything: the most extreme claim, the boldest visual, the question that opens a loop, the mid-action moment ("we lost everything" > "hi guys, today I want to talk about"). Read the whole transcript and the filmstrip LOOKING for it — it is almost never at the start of the raw footage.
- MOVE it: keep_segments reorders — open on the hook sentence or shot, then jump back to the natural start. A cold open into mid-sentence energy beats any intro. Never open on: a fade from black, a logo, a breath, "so basically", a wide establishing shot with nothing happening, or silence.
- The FIRST FRAME is the thumbnail on a paused player: it must be sharp, composed, and captioned (captions.md's first-frame rule) — look_at(output_times=[0.2]) and judge it.
- SAY THE HOOK BACK: when you deliver the edit, one clause names what you opened on — the user should recognize the choice.

OPEN LOOPS KEEP PEOPLE. A hook that ASKS ("this mistake cost me $40k") must not be answered until the end; if the raw footage answers it immediately, move the answer late (keep_segments). Payoff withheld = watch time. But never bait without payoff — the answer must actually arrive, or the comment section turns.

PATTERN INTERRUPTS — change the visual contract when the viewer's attention has a REASON to reset: the argument turns, a concrete noun needs proof, a reveal lands, energy rises, the speaker changes register, or the current composition has stopped yielding information. A punch-in, B-roll beat, caption emphasis, freeze-frame, or angle/crop switch can mark that event; stillness can mark it too. ONE device at a time, chosen by what the sentence is doing. Diagnose dead stretches by missing information, contrast or anticipation—not a stopwatch. A long revealing reaction can retain better than five arbitrary changes; a dense list may earn several fast ones.

MIDDLE DISCIPLINE: every sentence earns its place or dies. The test is not "is it bad" but "does anything change if it goes" — if nothing changes, cut it (cutting.md owns the mechanics). Repetition, hedging, throat-clearing, second takes: gone. A 34s reel that keeps moving beats a 58s reel with the same content.

END ON THE LOOP. Short-form replays: land the last cut ON the last word or the beat, no fade, no dead tail, no "thanks for watching" — the harder the ending cuts, the cleaner the loop back to your (strong) first frame. The best endings make replay seamless: final frame visually rhymes with the first. Long-form earns a real outro and a fade; reels never.

CTA: use when the brief or your editorial judgment benefits from one; a single short line just before the end is usually cleaner than a stack, and it should not cover the payoff moment.
